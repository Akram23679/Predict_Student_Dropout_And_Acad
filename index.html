Predicting student dropout: spotting students at risk before they leave.

01 / project brief

Identify at-risk students early enough to help.

Dropout is costly for students and institutions alike. The project builds classification models that predict each student's outcome and identifies which factors most strongly separate dropouts from graduates, so support can be targeted earlier.

4,424student records
34input features
3outcome classes
6models compared
Project questionsScope
  • Prediction: can a student's outcome (Dropout, Enrolled, Graduate) be predicted reliably?
  • Model choice: which algorithm performs best on imbalanced, three-class data?
  • Drivers: which academic, financial and demographic factors matter most?
  • Action: how can the results support an early-warning system?
Project deliverablesAnalysis
  • Jupyter notebook covering inspection, EDA, modelling and evaluation.
  • Exploratory charts: outcome mix, correlations, academic and macroeconomic patterns.
  • Six trained classifiers with cross-validated tuning for KNN and SVM.
  • Model comparison, feature importance and recommendations.
02 / data and method

Clean data, a fair split and leak-free tuning.

The dataset is Predict Students' Dropout and Academic Success (Realinho et al., UCI Machine Learning Repository). Every feature is numeric, with no missing values or duplicate rows.

Data and featuresInputs
  • Enrolment: application mode and order, course, attendance, previous qualification.
  • Background: age, gender, nationality, parents' qualifications and occupations.
  • Financial: debtor status, tuition fees up to date, scholarship holder.
  • Academic: 1st- and 2nd-semester curricular units credited, enrolled, evaluated, approved and grades.
  • Macroeconomic: unemployment rate, inflation rate and GDP.
MethodPipeline
  1. Loaded the CSV with BOM handling and checked types, missing values and duplicates.
  2. Explored distributions, correlations and outcome patterns with Seaborn.
  3. Split 80/20 with stratification (3,539 train / 885 test students).
  4. Wrapped scale-sensitive models in a StandardScaler pipeline to avoid data leakage.
  5. Tuned KNN's k and SVM's C and gamma with 5-fold stratified cross-validation on training data only.
  6. Scored every model on the held-out test set with accuracy and macro F1.

05 / findings

Where the data points to risk.

Findings combine model results with exploratory analysis. Group differences are associations in this dataset and should not be read as proof of cause.

77.7%

Three models effectively tie

Tuned SVM (77.7%), Random Forest (77.4%) and Logistic Regression (77.3%) are within 4 predictions of each other on 885 test students. KNN and a single Decision Tree are clearly weaker.

0.43

Enrolled is the hardest class

Even the best model recalls only 43% of Enrolled students, most of whom it predicts as Graduate. This is why macro F1 (0.71) sits well below accuracy.

1.9 vs 6.2

Semester results lead everything

Dropouts pass an average of 1.9 units in the 2nd semester versus 6.2 for graduates, with grades of 5.9 vs 12.7. Approved units and grades are the four strongest predictors.

86.6%

Overdue fees signal dropout

86.6% of students whose tuition fees were not up to date dropped out, compared with 24.7% of those who were current. 62% of debtors also dropped out.

76%

Scholarships align with graduation

76% of scholarship holders graduated, against 41% of other students, and only 12% dropped out.

57.6%

Older entrants are more at risk

Students who enrolled aged 25–29 dropped out at 57.6%, compared with 21.2% for those aged 17–20. Macroeconomic indicators ranked low as predictors.

06 / recommendations

Six actions for an early-warning system.

Recommendations cover how an institution could use the findings and how the model itself could be improved.

01

Flag risk after the 1st semester

Use approved units and grades to trigger tutoring, academic advising and check-ins for students who fall behind early.

02

Monitor fees and debt

Treat overdue tuition as an early alert. Offer payment plans, hardship funds and financial counselling before students disengage.

03

Expand financial support

Review scholarship reach and eligibility, since scholarship holders show much stronger graduation outcomes.

04

Support mature students

Provide flexible scheduling, evening options and targeted mentoring for students who enrol at 25 or older.

05

Improve the Enrolled class

Try class_weight="balanced", resampling and gradient boosting (e.g. HistGradientBoosting) to lift recall on the minority class.

06

Build an enrolment-time model

Train a version on features known at admission only, so risk can be flagged before any semester results exist.

07 / limitations and next steps

Interpret the results within the data's limits.

The models are a strong baseline, but several factors should be addressed before using them to make decisions about real students.

Limitations
  • Data comes from one higher-education institution, so results may not transfer elsewhere.
  • The strongest features are semester results, which are only available after students have started.
  • Categorical codes (course, occupation) were used as numbers rather than one-hot encoded.
  • Demographic and financial patterns are associations, not causes.
Suggested next steps
  • One-hot encode categorical features and compare results.
  • Test gradient boosting and class-balanced models.
  • Check fairness: compare error rates across gender and age groups.
  • Validate on newer cohorts before using predictions operationally.
